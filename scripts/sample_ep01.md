| 0:20 | Backstory | TIMELINE: 1951 talks begin · 1960 signed in Karachi · 1965, 1971, 1999 conflicts (treaty survives) · April 2025 abeyance · Aug 2026 Court of Arbitration award. | The Indus Waters Treaty was signed in Karachi in September 1960 by Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Pakistan's President Ayub Khan, after about nine years of talks brokered by the World Bank. It survived the wars of 1965 and 1971, and the Kargil conflict of 1999. Then, in April 2025, after a terror attack in Pahalgam killed twenty-six people, India announced that the treaty would be held in abeyance. |
| 1:05 | What's happening | BOARD: the camera flies in from the official map of India (Survey of India, credited) to the north-west; six river ribbons flow (moving dashes): the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) light up in teal, "India"; the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) in coral, "Pakistan". Then a three-step ladder: Commission → Neutral Expert → Court of Arbitration. | The treaty divides the six rivers of the Indus basin. The three eastern rivers, the Ravi, the Beas and the Sutlej, are for India's unrestricted use. The three western rivers, the Indus, the Jhelum and the Chenab, go to Pakistan, though India may use them in limited ways, such as run-of-the-river hydropower. The treaty also set up a Permanent Indus Commission, with one commissioner from each country, and a three-step way to settle disputes: the Commission, then a Neutral Expert, then a Court of Arbitration. |
| 1:40 | What's happening | EXPLAIN: dam icons on the Chenab ribbon labelled "sediment flushing"; project tags "Pakal Dul" and "Ratle"; a tribunal icon with "Court of Arbitration · interim award · 31 Aug 2026"; the Ratle tag gets a small "limits for now" note. | Since April 2025, India has flushed sediment from dams on the Chenab and sped up hydropower projects such as Pakal Dul and Ratle. Then, on the thirty-first of August 2026, a Court of Arbitration under the treaty issued an interim award. It said India's grounds were not enough to suspend the treaty, and it limited some construction at Ratle for now. |
| 2:05 | Where they stand | STANDS: two equal cards. Left "India says": "In abeyance until Pakistan credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism" · "Court set up in breach of the treaty; no jurisdiction; India never appeared". Right "Pakistan says": "Treaty remains fully in force and binding" · "India must comply with the treaty and its dispute decisions". | Here is where each side stands. India says the treaty stays in abeyance until Pakistan credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism. It says the court was set up in breach of the treaty and has no jurisdiction, and India has never appeared before it. Pakistan says the award shows the treaty remains fully in force and binding, and that India must comply with it. |
| 2:35 | India's stake | STAKES: three tiles. Shield "Security" · turbine "Energy for Jammu and Kashmir" · two river arrows "Upstream and downstream". | What is India's stake? First, security: India links the treaty to its fight against cross-border terrorism. Second, energy: fuller use of the western rivers means more hydropower for Jammu and Kashmir. Third, precedent: India is downstream of China on the Brahmaputra, so it has its own interest in how river agreements are read. |
| 3:05 | Passport stamp | STAMP: a passport page; the violet stamp thunks down: "EAST RBS → INDIA · WEST IJC → PAKISTAN · 1960 · WORLD BANK". | Here is your passport stamp. East is R-B-S: Ravi, Beas, Sutlej, for India. West is I-J-C: Indus, Jhelum, Chenab, for Pakistan. And the year is nineteen sixty, with the World Bank as the broker. |
| 3:30 | Exam desk | EXAM: statement card 1 flips to FALSE with its reason; card 2 flips to TRUE. Then a Mains card with a three-part frame. | Exam desk. Statement one: the treaty gives India unrestricted use of the Chenab. False. The Chenab is a western river; India's unrestricted rights are on the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej. Statement two: the treaty was brokered by the World Bank. True. In Mains, you might be asked whether water can be an instrument of foreign policy, with reference to the Indus Waters Treaty. Answer in three parts: the treaty and its record, India's security argument and Pakistan's legal argument, and the options ahead, from renegotiation to restoration. |

On the relations web, a new thread joins India and Pakistan: Indus Waters, in abeyance. Next briefing: India and Pakistan after Pahalgam. |
