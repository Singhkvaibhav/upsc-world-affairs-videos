After nine long years of talks, brokered by the World Bank, Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru and Pakistan's President Ayub Khan signed the treaty in Karachi, that September. And here's what makes it remarkable. It survived the wars of 1965 and 1971. It survived Kargil in 1999. But in April 2025, after a terror attack in Pahalgam killed twenty-six people, India took a step it had never taken before: it announced the treaty would be held in abeyance. |
| 1:05 | What's happening | BOARD: the camera flies in from the official map of India (Survey of India, credited) to the north-west; six river ribbons flow (moving dashes): the three eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej) light up in teal, "India"; the three western rivers (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) in coral, "Pakistan". Then a three-step ladder: Commission → Neutral Expert → Court of Arbitration. | So what does the treaty actually do? It splits six rivers. The three eastern rivers, the Ravi, the Beas and the Sutlej, are India's to use without limits. The three western ones, the Indus, the Jhelum and the Chenab, go to Pakistan. India can still use them, but only in limited ways, like run-of-the-river hydropower. It also built a system to settle disputes. Step one: the Permanent Indus Commission, with one commissioner from each country. Step two: a Neutral Expert. Step three: a Court of Arbitration. |
| 1:40 | What's happening | EXPLAIN: dam icons on the Chenab ribbon labelled "sediment flushing"; project tags "Pakal Dul" and "Ratle"; a tribunal icon with "Court of Arbitration · interim award · 31 Aug 2026"; the Ratle tag gets a small "limits for now" note. | So, what has India done since? From April 2025, it has flushed sediment from its dams on the Chenab, and put hydropower projects like Pakal Dul and Ratle on the fast track. Then came the twist. On the thirty-first of August 2026, the Court of Arbitration issued an interim award. Its message: India's grounds were not enough to suspend the treaty. And for now, it limited some construction at Ratle. |
| 2:05 | Where they stand | STANDS: two equal cards. Left "India says": "In abeyance until Pakistan credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism" · "Court set up in breach of the treaty; no jurisdiction; India never appeared". Right "Pakistan says": "Treaty remains fully in force and binding" · "India must comply with the treaty and its dispute decisions". | So, who says what? India's position is firm: the treaty stays in abeyance until Pakistan credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism. India says this court was set up in breach of the treaty, has no jurisdiction, and India has never appeared before it. Pakistan sees it the other way. It says the award shows the treaty is fully in force and binding, and that India must comply. |
| 2:35 | India's stake | STAKES: three tiles. Shield "Security" · turbine "Energy for Jammu and Kashmir" · two river arrows "Upstream and downstream". | Now, why should you care? Because three big things are at stake for India. One: security. India ties this treaty directly to cross-border terrorism. Two: energy. Fuller use of the western rivers means more hydropower for Jammu and Kashmir. And three: precedent. On the Brahmaputra, India is the downstream country, below China. So how river treaties are read matters to India too. |
| 3:05 | Passport stamp | STAMP: a passport page; the violet stamp thunks down: "EAST RBS → INDIA · WEST IJC → PAKISTAN · 1960 · WORLD BANK". | Okay, memory time! Here's your passport stamp. | 3:30 | Exam desk | EXAM: statement card 1 flips to FALSE with its reason; card 2 flips to TRUE. Then a Mains card with a three-part frame. | Exam desk! True or false: the treaty gives India unrestricted use of the Chenab. Think about it. False! The Chenab is a western river. India's unrestricted rights are on the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej. Next one: the treaty was brokered by the World Bank. True! Easy marks, if you remember the stamp. And for Mains, practise this one: can water be an instrument of foreign policy? Discuss with reference to the Indus Waters Treaty. Build your answer in three parts. First, the treaty and its record. Second, India's security argument and Pakistan's legal argument. Third, the options ahead, from renegotiation to restoration. |
